No-input mixing

A mixing desk is built to combine sounds that come from somewhere else. A no-input mixer has nothing plugged in. Its outputs are patched back into its own inputs, the loop starts to oscillate, and the desk becomes the sound source.

How it works

Once a channel hears itself, every control changes meaning. Gain decides whether a loop stays silent, sings or screams. The equaliser, which normally shapes a sound, now sets its pitch and timbre. Routing between channels and effects decides how loops pull at each other. Small moves have large, sometimes sudden results: the player is never fully in control, and that is the point.

Short history

The idea is older than the name. Composers worked with electronic feedback through the 1960s and 70s. In Robert Ashley's The Wolfman (1964) a singer shapes feedback between a microphone and the hall's speakers. David Tudor's Untitled (1972) chains components "linked together with multiple feedback loops", with no outside sound. In Alvin Lucier's Bird and Person Dyning (1975) the performer wears microphones in the ears. From 1987 David Lee Myers recorded as Arcane Device with home-built "feedback machines".

The mixer became an instrument in its own right in Tokyo. Toshimaru Nakamura first used a mixer to extend his guitar; in 1997 he unplugged the guitar and kept the mixer. He called it the no-input mixing board, because there is "no external sound source". His albums No-Input Mixing Board (2000) and No-Input Mixing Board 2 (2001) began a series that continues today. He was part of onkyo, a quiet, texture-led improvised music; on Good Morning Good Night (2004) he plays with Sachiko M on sine waves and Otomo Yoshihide on turntables.

Who plays it

Many have followed. Marko Ciciliani wrote Mask (2002), a composed piece for no-input mixer. Andrea Neumann brings mixer feedback into her inside-piano. Philip White, Simon Grab, Richard Knight and many others use it in noise, improvisation and dance music.

The sound sits close to the music of Ryoji Ikeda and Alva Noto. Neither plays a no-input mixer, but records such as Ikeda's +/- (1996) are built from the same raw material feedback throws up: sine tones, clicks and noise, only made by design rather than by chance.

Feedback and rhythm

Feedback does not have to drift. Boost the lows and a loop settles into a steady pulse. Richard Knight makes minimal techno this way; Simon Grab drives his mixer from a step sequencer; Void Hands make "analogue techno using only mixers and feedback". Industrial and power-electronics artists have long used mixer feedback for its bass and its pulse.

Lilliput builds this into the instrument. A rhythm engine gates, chokes or strikes 6 loops in time, and a 4-voice drum synthesizer (kick, snare, hats and metallic percussion) plays on the same tempo and beat. Feedback can stay a drone, or become noise inside a rhythm.
